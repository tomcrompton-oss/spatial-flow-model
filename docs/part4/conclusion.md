# Conclusion

## Where we started

Part 3 ended with one potential for the whole plane, space curved across it and the small corrections general
relativity gives: general relativity for bodies on given paths. Part 4 let the bodies move by the flow themselves,
which raised a question a light body never did: how does a body move in a flow that holds its own?

## What we built

[Each Body in the Others' Flow](each-body-in-the-others-flow.md) left each body's own flow out, and moved it in the
others' alone. To leave a body's own flow out it went back to a flow built from each body's own, which already fails
the Moon's de Sitter turn. With comparable masses it fails three more ways: the binary's orbit
turns 17% too fast at equal masses, the pair's centre accelerates by itself, and a falling pair has Nordtvedt's $\eta$
near 1. Curving space doubles every error. What it leaves out is a body's own field changing how the other's acts on
it.

[Moving With Its Own Space](moving-with-its-own-space.md) kept each body's flow, and moved each body with the space
round it, averaged over a small sphere: the rule general relativity's own two-body equations are worked out with. Round
the Earth, and round either body of a binary on any orbit tried, the averaged space moves with the body. For a
circular binary the rule gives general relativity's orbit exactly, as long as the flow keeps one correction the Moon
hardly needed: each body's potential carried at its velocity.

So the model that passed Part 3, with the bodies moving by it, is general relativity's two-body motion to the first
order beyond Newton: worked out exactly for a circular binary, and argued for the rest.

## What is left

Two things.

The first is to run it. The binary's turning is worked out exactly for circles, and on eccentric orbits the bodies
are shown to move with their own space; but no eccentric binary has been run through the whole model, orbit after
orbit, and neither has a falling pair. Those would turn the two "argued" rows into measurements.

The second is energy. A real binary loses energy to gravitational waves, and its orbit shrinks: the Hulse–Taylor
pulsar's by 3.5 metres a year. That is the order beyond what Parts 1 to 4 were built to, and it is where
[Part 5](../part5.md) begins.
