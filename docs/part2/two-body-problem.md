# The Two Body Problem

## Where we are

Part 1 took one idea: that space flows into a body at the escape speed, $u = \sqrt{2GM/r}$, and that clocks run slow
by their speed through that space. Adding only Newton's mechanics and special relativity, it gave Newton's gravity,
all four classic tests of general relativity, and black holes, all in agreement with exact general relativity. For a
single body, the flowing space *is* general relativity.

But a single body is the easy case. Space flows straight towards it, at a speed that depends only on the distance, and
symmetry settles everything else. The universe has more than one body in it.

## A second body

Add a second body, the same size as the first, and hold both still for now. (What keeps them apart doesn't matter
yet: this part of the story is about bodies at rest.) Each has its own gravitational potential, and near both, the
potentials add:

$$
\Phi = -\frac{GM}{r_1} - \frac{GM}{r_2}
$$

where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the distances to the two bodies. The hypothesis ties the speed of space to the potential:
$\tfrac12 u^2 = -\Phi$, so that clocks held still are slowed as general relativity says. Space should flow at

$$
u = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r_1} + \frac{2GM}{r_2}}
$$

and fall towards where the pull is, along Newton's $g$:

## Far away, or close to either body

From far away the pair looks like a single body of twice the mass. The potential is close to $-2GM/r$, and space
flows straight in towards the pair at close to $\sqrt{4GM/r}$, just as it would round one body. Everything in Part 1
carries over.

Close to either body, its own potential dominates. The other body adds a little to the speed of space and tips its
direction slightly, but the flow is nearly that body's own, straight in at nearly $\sqrt{2GM/r}$. Again, Part 1
carries over, with a small correction.

## The centre

The trouble is in between, and it is clearest at the centre, midway between the two bodies.

There, the two bodies pull equally in opposite directions, so $g$ is zero. Something let go at rest at the centre
stays where it is. But the potential there isn't zero: it is the two bodies' potentials added, deeper than anywhere
else at that distance from either body. General relativity says a clock held still at the centre runs slow by both
bodies' time dilation together. So, by the hypothesis, space at the centre must be moving past that clock, fast: at
$\sqrt{8GM/d}$, where $d$ is the distance between the bodies.

Moving in which direction? Nothing picks one. The two bodies are the same, so space has no reason to flow towards one
rather than the other, and no reason to flow up rather than down. The diagram shows the difficulty: just above and
below the centre, space comes in towards it; just to either side, it leaves towards the bodies. At the centre itself
it would have to be moving at full speed in all of those directions at once.

The obvious ways out both fail:

- **Add the two flows as vectors.** Each body's own flow, straight in towards it, is added to the other's. At the
  centre the two are equal and opposite, so they cancel, and space doesn't move. A clock held still there would then
  run at the rate of a clock far away, with no time dilation at all, which isn't what general relativity says. And
  away from the centre, the speed of the added flows no longer matches the potential, so space no longer falls at
  Newton's $g$.
- **Keep the speed and point it along $g$.** That is the diagram. It gives the right speed everywhere, and the right
  direction near either body, but at the centre $g$ is zero and has no direction to give.

With one body, the speed of space and its direction came from the same place: the body. With two, they come apart.
The potential says how fast space must move, but nothing says which way. That is the two body problem, and the rest
of Part 2 is about how to resolve it.
